# Council transcript: repair the old laptop or replace it?

*An invented example. Run 2026-09-12 19:30. Advisors and reviewers on Sonnet, the chair on Opus.*

## The original question

"My laptop is seven years old, the battery is gone and it's full. Fix it or buy a new one?"

## The framed question

The family laptop is seven years old. The battery lasts forty minutes and the 128 GB drive is full. It is used for
email, photos, video calls and a school portal. A repair shop can fit a new battery and a larger drive. A refurbished
replacement costs about three times the likely repair; a new one about five times. Nothing else is wrong with it yet.
Repair, replace refurbished, or replace new? The worst case is paying twice within a year.

## The advisors

**The Contrarian.** A seven-year-old laptop is past the age where things fail one at a time. Fix the battery and the
hinge goes next, then the screen. You may pay for a repair and then a replacement anyway, and you will have spent the
weeks in between nursing it. Ask the shop what else they see when it is open. If they hesitate, do not repair.

**The First Principles Thinker.** What does the household actually need from this machine? Email, photos, calls and
one portal. None of that needs a fast computer. The real problems are two specific ones: power and space. Solve the
two problems, not the category "laptop". Move the photos to an external drive and the space problem is gone today.

**The Expansionist.** Replace it, and keep the old one as the family photo backup that never leaves the house. Then
the new machine starts clean, and the photo problem is solved for good instead of moved to a bigger drive.

**The Outsider.** If I only read this: it works, except the battery and the space. Fix those two things. Why is buying
a whole new one even on the table?

**The Executor.** Getting a quote takes a day. Shopping for a new laptop takes three weeks of open tabs. Book the
shop's free check this week, get the quote in writing, and ask what else they found. Decide with the quote in hand.

## Peer review (anonymised; the mapping is at the end)

**Reviewer 1.** Strongest: E, because it turns the question into one action. Biggest blind spot: C, which spends more
to solve a problem the household did not ask about. All missed: where the photos live now, and whether they are
backed up at all.

**Reviewer 2.** Strongest: B, which shrinks the problem to two fixable things. Blind spot: A assumes failure is likely
without asking the age of the other parts. All missed: the school portal's browser requirements.

**Reviewer 3.** Strongest: A. It is the only one that prices the second failure. Blind spot: D, which ignores that the
machine is seven years old. All missed: the backup.

**Reviewer 4.** Strongest: E. Blind spot: C again. All missed: a date to look at it again.

**Reviewer 5.** Strongest: B. Blind spot: A, which gives no way to check its own worry. All missed: the backup, and a
date.

Mapping: A = the Contrarian, B = the First Principles Thinker, C = the Expansionist, D = the Outsider, E = the Executor.

## The chair's synthesis

**Where the council agrees.** The only two real problems are power and space. A written quote comes before anything
is bought.

**Where the council clashes.** The Contrarian expects the next failure soon; the Outsider and the First Principles
Thinker see a working machine with two fixable faults. Both are reasonable. The shop's look inside settles it.

**Blind spots the council caught.** Nobody had asked whether the photos are backed up. Three reviewers raised it. It
matters more than the laptop. And nobody set a date to look again.

**The call.** Repair it now, put the replacement money aside, and set a date to look again in March. Back the photos
up to an external drive this week, whatever the shop says.

**The one thing to do first.** Book the repair shop's free check this week and get the quote in writing.
